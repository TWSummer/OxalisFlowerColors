01 / The field guide

Four colors. Look between the lines.

Score the upper petal surface of an open, fresh flower. Ignore the veins, yellow throat, anthers, and petal underside.

!

A purple-veined white flower is still white. Faint color is not automatically white, and pale purple is not automatically pink. If the background hue cannot be separated from lighting or camera color cast, leave the color field unset and record why in the review log.

One useful pattern trait, kept deliberately simple.

The paper describes variation in purple venation, especially among white flowers. Test vein contrast as an independent photographic trait. It is a project proposal, not a classification validated by the paper.

Assess at a displayed flower diameter of 300 px, using a source flower at least 300 px across. Require three clear upper petals. Do not sharpen, recolor, or upscale. The full protocol defines the threshold and pilot acceptance criteria.

03 / A repeatable method

Good data starts
with knowing when
not to label.

Use the same written rules for people and agents. Keep an audit trail of every decision, including skips.

Download agent instructions ↓Read the complete protocol →
  1. 01

    Find the flower

    Check all photos. Leaves, closed buds, backs of flowers, and unusable close-ups cannot establish petal color. Flower annotations can help prioritize review, but are not a complete inventory.

  2. 02

    Score what you can see

    Choose white, blue, purple, or pink from the interveinal background. Keep species and location out of the color decision. Different flower colors in one record require review, not majority voting.

  3. 03

    Keep the evidence

    Log the photo ID, visible flower, color decision, confidence, and reason. Preserve existing values. Validate the pilot before scaling; record the verified field value after a write.

The research behind the atlas

Inspired by a taxonomic study.
Built for an ongoing observation project.

Brinegar’s study used four subjective color categories and 1,078 Research Grade iNaturalist flower images from 2015–2019. Figure 1 shows variation within those categories, including vein intensity among white flowers. It is not a 16-type pattern key.

Our atlas follows current iNaturalist identifications and all observation dates. Camera color, uneven sampling, cultivation, and taxonomic changes affect interpretation. A map of labels cannot independently test a species boundary that was itself identified using color.
