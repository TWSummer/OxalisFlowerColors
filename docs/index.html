01 / The field guide

Three colors. Four ways to look.

Score each trait independently on fresh, open flowers. These are our project categories, with Figure 1 as a visual reference.

Compare real flower close-ups →

i

Our own reading of the reference plate. Pink A–F; Purple G–H; White I–P. These assignments intentionally differ from the paper’s groups. The drawings below illustrate the rules; they are not photographs or measured color standards. See Brinegar (2022), Figure 1, p. 308.

Field: . Look between the veins, outside the yellow/orange throat and any white center ring.

A purple-veined white flower is White. A flower with pink base and edges remains Pink even if its middle is nearly white, as in F. Pale cool-looking I–L are White in this project; there is no Blue category.

Field: . Judge how strongly the vein lines stand out against the nearby background, not their hue or branching.

Our complete Figure 1 key: High O, P; Medium A, F, H; Low B, C, D, E, G, I, J, K, L, M, N. Assess a flower at a 300 px display diameter from a source at least 300 px across.

Field: . Inspect the transition just outside the yellow/orange throat on at least three visible petals.

Cannot be determined: an all-white flower with no distinguishable outer ring boundary, a hidden or angled center, glare, or an unresolved transition. Faint means a visible weak ring; it is not a substitute for uncertainty. Do not label a ring Absent unless the petal bases are clearly exposed.

Photo comparisons: 294114815 — Faint (subtle basal paling); 347719180 — Cannot be determined (cupped, oblique center). No Figure 1 panel is assigned as a Faint anchor.

Field: . Look for changes in pigment intensity within the broad areas between veins on the same petal.

Exclude the yellow/orange throat, vein lines, any distinct white center ring, shadows, glare, folds, and damage. A center ring alone does not increase variation: B and G remain None. Low is a written intermediate category; no plate photo has been designated its anchor.

03 / A repeatable method

Good data starts
with a shared
way of looking.

Use the same written rules for people and agents. Keep an audit trail of every decision, including skips.

Download agent instructions ↓Read the complete protocol →
  1. 01

    Find the flower

    Check all photos. Leaves, closed buds, backs of flowers, and unusable close-ups cannot establish petal color. Flower annotations can help prioritize review, but are not a complete inventory.

  2. 02

    Score what you can see

    Score color, vein contrast, white center ring, and pigment variation separately. Use Cannot be determined for unresolved traits. Different outcomes among flowers require review, not majority voting.

  3. 03

    Keep the evidence

    Log the photos examined, focal flower, each trait’s decision, and its reason. Preserve existing values. Re-read iNaturalist after each write and record the returned field value.

The research behind the atlas

Inspired by a taxonomic study.
Built for an ongoing observation project.

Brinegar’s study used four subjective color categories and 1,078 Research Grade iNaturalist flower images from 2015–2019. Our project deliberately regroups Figure 1 into three colors and adds three separate visual traits. This new key has not yet been assessed for agreement between independent reviewers.

Our atlas follows current iNaturalist identifications and all observation dates. Camera color, uneven sampling, cultivation, and taxonomic changes affect interpretation. A map of labels cannot independently test a species boundary that was itself identified using color.
